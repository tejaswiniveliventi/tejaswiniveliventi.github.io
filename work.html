01

Unjunk

Finding cleaner alternatives to the packaged foods we love.

Product AI Data

I wanted to build something that solved an actual problem rather than another project that existed mainly to help my career.

I joined the SheVibes cohort in May, working alongside product designers and other builders. That environment made me want to think about the product first: what is something people might actually find useful?

The idea was simple. We all have packaged foods we crave even when we know they're not particularly good choices. What if you could search for one and quickly find a cleaner alternative available where you live?

The problem I ran into

My first approach to recommendations didn't work as well as I expected. The model would find products that were technically alternatives but weren't necessarily cleaner.

For example, different types of packaged snacks can share many of the same basic ingredients. Simply asking an LLM for an "alternative" wasn't enough to distinguish a genuinely better option from another version of the same thing.

Changing the recommendation approach

I changed the prompt so that the model had a more specific definition of what I was looking for. Instead of just asking it to find alternatives, I asked it to consider the ingredients I wanted to avoid and the characteristics I wanted to see in the replacement.

I also wanted the recommendation to be explainable. The product should show why it was considered cleaner, including the ingredients that contributed to the assessment rather than presenting a mysterious score.

Open Food Facts provided product and ingredient data, while the NOVA classification helped provide a framework for thinking about processing.

One more problem: where is it sold?

A product available in Bangalore isn't necessarily available in Hyderabad. I could have used device GPS to determine a user's location, but I deliberately chose not to.

Instead, the user chooses their city. It gives the application enough information to make a useful recommendation without asking for more personal information or device permissions than necessary.

What I built

The application takes a food or brand name, analyzes the product information, finds potential cleaner alternatives, and presents the reasoning behind the recommendations along with places where the product can be purchased.

What I learned

The most interesting part wasn't getting an LLM to produce recommendations. It was realizing how much the quality of the result depended on defining what a good recommendation actually meant.

Building the product also forced me to think about small decisions that are easy to overlook: availability varies by location, "cleaner" needs a definition, and collecting more user information isn't automatically better.

02

Chat Analyzer

Exploring conversations, privacy, and local LLMs.

Python Data LLMs

I have too many WhatsApp group chats. Some of them seem to come alive exactly when I'm asleep.

I wanted to see whether I could turn exported chat conversations into something easier to understand: who was talking, when conversations were most active, what words and emojis appeared most often, and whether an LLM could give me a useful summary.

One constraint: keep the data local

I didn't want to upload personal conversations to a cloud service just to analyze them. So I decided that the experiment would process the conversations locally.

I used Ollama to run a language model on my own machine. The tradeoff became obvious pretty quickly: local processing gave me the privacy I wanted, but it also made the system considerably slower and limited the models I could comfortably run.

Cleaning the data

Before analyzing anything, I cleaned and anonymized the exported conversation data. The application then extracts useful conversation-level information such as participant activity, message frequency, word usage, emojis, and activity over time.

The result is a small Streamlit application where conversations can be explored through charts and summary statistics.

An unexpected experiment

Eventually I took an anonymized portion of the same conversation data and used it with ChatGPT so I could compare the summaries.

The difference was interesting. Ollama could produce useful summaries, but the same prompt sometimes produced noticeably different results and important parts of the discussion could be missed.

The ChatGPT results were more consistent and better at capturing the context of the conversation.

What I learned

This experiment gave me a practical lesson about model selection that I couldn't have gotten just from reading about LLMs.

A good prompt isn't enough. The model itself matters, particularly for tasks like summarization where context, consistency, and the ability to identify what's important can make a big difference.

It also made the tradeoff between privacy and capability very tangible: keeping data local can be valuable, but it comes with real constraints in compute, speed, and model quality.
